It starts small. It always does.

You open your phone in the morning — before you've even had water, before your brain has fully loaded — and someone from your batch has already posted about their internship. You close the app. You open it again twenty minutes later. Someone else got into a program you applied for. You put the phone face down on the bed and stare at the ceiling.

You tell yourself it doesn't bother you.

It bothers you.

This is the comparison trap. And before you dismiss this as another "just believe in yourself" article written by someone who has clearly never had to survive an Indian college system — let me be upfront. This isn't motivational content. This is just what's actually happening, backed by data, told without the sugarcoating.

## The Numbers Nobody Reads Out Loud

**46% of Indian college students** are carrying clinically significant anxiety right now. Not "stressed about exams" anxiety. Clinically. Significant.

A 2025 study surveyed 1,628 students aged 18–29 across eight major Indian cities. Nearly **70% had moderate to high anxiety**. 60% had moderate to high depression. This isn't a niche finding from one struggling college. This is the baseline across tier-1 cities — the places everyone is trying to get into.

India is the **2nd loneliest country in the world**. **57%** of young Indians now use AI as their closest confidant — not a friend, not a sibling, not a roommate. An AI. And **61%** of people aged 18–25 globally report what researchers are calling "profound loneliness."

Meanwhile, open Instagram. Everyone looks fine.

That's not a coincidence. That's a design.

Every platform you use is algorithmically optimized to show you the moments that make you feel behind — because inadequacy keeps you scrolling, and scrolling keeps the lights on for someone else. You are not the user. You are the product being slowly convinced that you are not enough.

<HighlightBox variant="info" title="The Design">
  Research confirms: both passive scrolling *and* active posting are linked to increased loneliness over time. It doesn't matter if you're consuming or performing. Digital interaction at that volume makes you feel more alone — not less.
</HighlightBox>

## The Specific Indian Flavor of This Problem

Research that actually looked at Indian Reddit users posting about [**mental health**](/blog/loneliness-check) found something important.

In the West, online mental health discussions are mostly about **anxiety and the past**. In India, they're about **sadness, the present moment, and work and achievement.** Indian users aren't just struggling with generic sadness. They're struggling with the feeling of *falling behind right now.*

It makes sense when you zoom out. 1.5 million students register for [**JEE**](/blog/degree-vs-ai) in a single year. The system, by design, produces more losers than winners — not because the students are less capable, but because the seats are limited and the stakes are framed as everything. You either get in or you don't. You either make it or you don't. There's no language for "I'm figuring it out at my own pace" in a culture that measures worth in ranks and percentiles.

And then you add social media on top of that pressure cooker.

Instagram didn't create the fire. It handed everyone a megaphone to broadcast their wins, and an algorithm to make sure you see every single one of them in the exact moment you're feeling most uncertain about yourself.

## What the Comparison Trap Actually Does to You

It doesn't hit you like a truck. That's the thing.

It's the slow accumulation of a thousand small moments where you measured yourself against someone else and came up short. The classmate who got placed first. The friend-of-a-friend who started a company at 19. The person you went to school with who seems to have everything figured out.

You start pulling back. You stop applying for things because the fear of failing publicly feels worse than never trying. You stop talking about your struggles because everyone around you seems to be doing fine. You start performing okayness.

And here's the brutal irony: **80% of Gen Z** globally reported feeling lonely in the past year. Only **15%** say they've *never* felt lonely.

If nearly everyone feels this way, the problem is not you. The problem is a shared hallucination — a collective fiction that everyone else is doing better, held in place by the fact that nobody wants to be the first one to admit they're struggling.

## What Escaping It Actually Looks Like

Not a detox. Not a 30-day challenge. Not a vision board.

Someone once posted on Reddit about preparing for competitive exams and described the hardest day of the entire experience. It wasn't the day they failed a mock test. It was the day they saw a classmate's rank — and for the first time, felt genuinely happy for them, without the familiar sting of comparison underneath it.

They said they didn't know when the shift happened. It crept up slowly. It came from getting so exhausted by the scoreboard that they just... stopped checking it. Not from discipline. From fatigue. From finally deciding that someone else's highlight reel was not useful information about their own life.

That's what the exit from the comparison trap actually looks like. Not a switch. A slow, deliberate withdrawal. A quiet decision that you are going to measure yourself against who you were last month — not against who someone else is on their best day.

The students who come out of college mentally intact aren't the ones who optimized hardest. They're the ones who figured out earlier that the race was set up in a way that was never going to feel fair — and built [**something of their own**](/blog/side-hustle-era) instead of chasing someone else's finish line.

<HighlightBox variant="tip" title="One Honest Question">
  The next time you feel the urge to compare — and you will, because we all do — ask yourself one thing: *Whose life am I actually trying to build here? Mine, or a version of someone else's that only exists on a screen?*
</HighlightBox>

You already know the answer. You've always known.

The hard part is choosing to act like it.
